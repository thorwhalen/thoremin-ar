# Related work: AR/VR/MR instruments and air-drawing music

*Research note, checked 2026-10-25. Part of the thoremin-ar series. It does not repeat the prior art on air guitar, drums, flute and bass, nor the latency budget, which thoremin already has in [`docs/research/air-instruments.md`](https://github.com/thorwhalen/thoremin/blob/main/docs/research/air-instruments.md) (cited as AI) and [`docs/research/compendium.md`](https://github.com/thorwhalen/thoremin/blob/main/docs/research/compendium.md) (cited as COMP, chapter 3 on latency). Here: the XR instrument literature, the commercial and open apps, the lineage of drawn sound, and what all of it says to a webcam-and-MediaPipe instrument that wants to "draw and move objects" and later run on glasses.*

## In short

- The field has a name and a survey. Serafin et al. (2016) frame VR musical instruments as a three-layer design problem (interaction mapping, VR-specific qualities, overall goals) and propose nine guidelines [1][2]. NIME has since added AR work (Luna on Quest 2, 2025 [3]), XR-instrument studies [4][5] and 3D musical drawing [6]. I could not obtain the full text of the nine guidelines; this note quotes only what secondary sources and the abstract support.
- Almost every commercial XR instrument is **direct and spatial**: grab, poke, strike or pinch a virtual object. Very few are **indirect** (draw, then play back). The drawn-sound lineage (Oramics 1957-63, UPIC 1977, Hyperscore 2001, Kandinsky, Paint with Music) is the opposite: indirect, time-on-x, pitch-on-y. Thoremin sits where the two meet, and the gap between them is open ground.
- The one close cousin to thoremin's core already exists: Spatial Symphony on Vision Pro is a pinch-pitch / pinch-volume theremin [7], and Neo Theremin, Wavr and Handel are webcam-plus-MediaPipe-plus-Web-Audio instruments in the browser [8][9]. None draws.
- Tracking latency in consumer headsets is not better than a webcam pipeline: measured at about 45 ms (Quest 2, peer-reviewed) [10], about 70 ms (Quest 3) and about 128 ms (Vision Pro) in an informal frame-count test [11]. Thoremin's roughly 100 ms to half-pitch (COMP section 3.1) is the same order. The headset advantage is *visual registration* (virtual objects sit in the room), not speed.
- The recurring lessons: no haptics means the visual and audio response must carry the "contact"; sustained mid-air work fatigues the arm (Consumed Endurance [12]); tiered control (cheap gestures first, rich ones later) is what makes an instrument learnable [5]; and "playing a drawing" is best done as a looping playhead over strokes, with pitch quantisation optional.
- Meta display glasses opened to developers in a preview in May 2026 with EMG pinch input and a Web Apps path [13]; that is a possible future input surface for thoremin but is covered by other notes in this series.

## 1. Academic background

### 1.1 VR/XR musical instruments

Serafin, Erkut, Kojs, Nilsson and Nordahl give the field overview from the performer's side, and propose nine design guidelines, evaluation methods, case studies and open challenges [1]. A companion Audio Mostly paper presents the guidelines for multisensory interaction design [2]. The abstract does not list the guidelines; a secondary account says they stress feedback, mapping and minimal latency, and Çamcı et al. summarise the evaluation scheme as three layers: an interaction-design layer (input to output mapping), a VR-specific layer (intrinsic qualities of the virtual experience) and a layer of overall goals [5]. Geronazzo and Serafin note that high latency or jitter can impair performance and that impulsive interactions need lower latency than continuous ones [14]. This matches thoremin's own finding that strikes and glides have different budgets (COMP section 3.2).

Çamcı, Vilaplana Stark and Wang (NIME 2020) built four VIMEs (virtual interfaces for musical expression) and ran a study on immersion, control, learnability and physical effort [5]. Findings useful here: users forgave non-physical behaviour of virtual objects as long as it was consistent; the simplest interaction (grab and move) already gave "desirable results", with finer adjustments held back on controller buttons, which they call a tiered control scheme; and perceived physical effort did not track actual movement (a room-scale ball-throwing VIME ranked less effortful than a stand-still one). Graf and Barthet (QMUL) define XR musical instruments (XRMIs) as a research field and identify self-occlusion as the main failure of vision-based hand tracking in them, then show that adding surface EMG improves finger-joint estimation for occluded joints [4]. That is directly relevant to a single webcam, where occlusion is the dominant error (see AI section 2.4 for the 10.9 degree RMSE figure).

Van Kerrebroeck et al. built a "virtual drum circle" in XR between two remote players: drum-pad to speaker latency was 17 +/- 2 ms, but motion capture to HoloLens visualisation was 58 +/- 4 ms, so the visual lagged the sound by about 40 ms, a situation the authors note does not occur in real life [15]. Seeing a partner as a realistic avatar raised shared agency; music (versus a metronome) improved timing [15].

### 1.2 AR instruments on head-mounted displays

Luna (Dietz and Martin, NIME 2025) is an AR instrument on Meta Quest 2 built with libpd (Pure Data as a library); the authors report that Quest 2 hand tracking "was capable of complex gestural interactions", through autoethnographic development notes [3]. "Music Everywhere" (NIME 2017) was a HoloLens AR piano-improvisation tutor over a real electric piano [16]. "Cubing Sound" (Wang and Martin, 2022) is cited as a head-mounted AR NIME design [17]; I only saw it in a reading list. The pattern: AR instruments are either *teachers* anchored to a physical instrument (Music Everywhere, PianoVision) or *free-floating* hand-tracked objects (Luna).

### 1.3 Mid-air gesture music before headsets

The theremin is the template (thoremin's name), and Leap Motion was the 2013 wave: its store launched with 14 music apps, among them AirHarp (plucked-string synthesis, pluck by tapping down, strum by reaching further in), AirBeats (air drumming) and GECO, a $10 mapper from hand gestures to MIDI [18][19]. Han and Gold's NIME 2014 lessons on Leap instrument design is already in AI [20]. Mid-air haptics were tried early: Ciglar's Syntact (NIME 2010) produced audible and tactile sound with airborne ultrasound [21], and an ETRI AirPiano used ultrasonic vibration to simulate key resistance and improved the user experience in a study [22]. These remain lab or niche items; no mainstream XR instrument ships mid-air haptics.

### 1.4 Drawn sound and graphical sound

These are the ancestors of "play a drawing", and they all make the same choice: **time runs along one axis, pitch (or timbre) along the other, and the drawing is the score**.

- **Oramics** (Daphne Oram, conceived 1957, developed with a 1962 grant and engineer Graham Wrench, first fully drawn piece 1963): ten synchronised 35 mm film strips read by photocells; pitch "neumes" drawn on three strips drive a master oscillator, four strips carry volume graphs, two carry vibrato and reverb, and four glass slides carry drawn waveforms [23][24]. The composer set melody and timing first, then dynamics, then vibrato and reverb: layered passes, each drawn separately [24]. The machine was monophonic and non-real-time-performable in the instrument sense; it was a drawing-to-sound *composition* tool.
- **UPIC** (Xenakis and CEMAMu, first complete prototype reported as 1977; sources differ on dates): a large digitising tablet with a pen; arcs drawn on it become sound, the vertical axis is pitch character and the horizontal axis is time; users also drew waveforms and envelopes [25][26]. Early synthesis was not real-time. Xenakis's motive was passages too complex for staff notation [26].
- **Hyperscore** (Machover and MIT Media Lab, 2001, spun into a Fisher-Price toy): freehand coloured lines are translated into colour-coded motives, melodies and chords, and the program builds a complete piece; Machover's group also coined the term **hyperinstrument** (1986) for technology that gives extra power and finesse to virtuosic players, and since 1992 built instruments for amateurs [27][28].
- **Spectral painters** (MetaSynth, Photosounder): the image *is* a spectrogram, with time on x, frequency on y and brightness as amplitude [29].
- **Web toys**: Chrome Music Lab Kandinsky (draw lines, circles and triangles; height sets pitch; colour or shape picks the instrument; play loops the picture) [30], and Google's Paint with Music (2021, Magenta DDSP): stroke path becomes notes of a chosen instrument (violin, saxophone, trumpet, flute), vertical position gives pitch and drawing speed affects volume [31].
- **XR drawing**: Gruy and Berthaut's 3D musical drawing at Lille is the closest research match to what the user asked for. MagneTip (NIME 2024) draws sonic and visual paths in 3D with one or two hands and moves part of the interaction back into the physical space for "more direct audio and haptic feedback", combining co-located and spatialised feedback [6]. A NIME 2025 piece uses a controller as a paintbrush for notes placed along the stroke and as a lens that transforms what it frames [32]. I did not find a hand-tracking (controller-free) version of either.

## 3. Design lessons for thoremin

### 3.1 Latency tolerances: do not chase headset numbers

Headset hand tracking is not faster than thoremin. A peer-reviewed Quest 2 study measured an average temporal delay of about 45 ms with 1.1 cm fingertip error and 9.6 degrees of joint-angle error [10]; a Quest Pro robot test found about 177 ms at motion onset but about 28 ms once moving, suggesting the system predicts poses [43]; an informal frame count gave Quest 3 about 70 ms and Vision Pro about 128 ms (March 2024, not peer-reviewed) [11]. Thoremin's measured chain is about 100 ms to the pitch being half-way (COMP section 3.1), so the *tracking* gap to a headset is small. The targets (COMP section 3.2): 20 ms undetected and 30 ms detected on a theremin-like continuous instrument, and 10 ms for struck sounds.

What the audio-visual literature adds: people tolerate video lagging audio less than video leading audio; a commonly reported simultaneity window runs from about 50 ms audio-leading to 150 ms audio-lagging for speech, and for a hammer striking a peg about 75 ms audio lead to 188 ms audio lag, with simple flash-and-tone pairs tighter [44]. Practical reading: in an overlay (drawn object plus sound), **let the sound lead the picture**, never the reverse, and keep visual feedback within about 40 to 50 ms of the sound. The XR drum circle's 40 ms visual lag behind sound was a conscious compromise [15]. Passthrough latency also matters: Quest 3's roughly 39 ms of passthrough masks some of its tracking delay, so it feels snappier [11]; a camera-composite overlay on thoremin's canvas (`canvas-overlay`) has the same property, since the drawn guide and the video are in the same frame.

**Cost and fit.** Zero new cost for the sound path. For continuous drawn objects (a stroke played by a playhead) latency is easier than for strikes because the sound is scheduled from the stroke's stored time, not from the hand's current sample: the playhead is the clock, which is the one case in this note where thoremin's sub-frame machinery (COMP chapter 4) is not needed.

### 3.2 Absent haptics: visual and audio feedback must supply the contact

Nothing in the table gives touch except through hardware or props: PianoVision anchors to a real keyboard; MagneTip moves part of the interaction back into physical space [6][40]; ultrasound haptics exist in the lab [21][22]. A webcam has none of these. What survives: (1) an immediate, unmistakable audio onset on pinch or entry; (2) a visual state change on the object the moment contact is detected (colour, size, a ring) so the player sees the "click"; (3) a **visible hit region at the fingertip**, not the shaft or palm, after Paradiddle's stick-hitbox complaints [39]; (4) consistent rules, since Çamcı et al. found users accept non-physical behaviour if it is consistent [5]. Pinch has a natural proprioceptive "click" (thumb meets finger), which is why Spatial Symphony and Neo Theremin use it as the gate [7][8]. A drawn-object instrument in AR should prefer **pinch to grab and poke through a surface plane over free poking of a floating object**, for the same reason.

**Cost and fit.** A tiny node: a "contact" state with hysteresis (enter radius smaller than exit radius) between `hand-features` and the canvas overlay, emitting an event stream the existing graph can use.

### 3.3 Fatigue: design for rest

Mid-air interaction causes the gorilla-arm effect. Hincapié-Ramos et al. proposed Consumed Endurance, derived from upper-arm biomechanics, which correlates strongly with Borg CR10 perceived exertion and can be computed from a camera-based skeleton tracker; an open workbench computes it in real time [12]. A later metric (NICER) models above-shoulder work and recovery [45]. Two implications: (1) draw in the **comfortable zone** (below shoulder, elbow bent, near the body); a webcam sitting on a desk makes this natural, glasses with a head-fixed view do not; (2) thoremin could *measure its own fatigue cost*, since it already has the body skeleton, and show a gentle prompt or shrink the active region. Çamcı et al. also found that effort is perceived, not only mechanical [5], so engaging feedback and pacing matter as well as geometry.

### 3.4 Discoverability: tiered control, modes, and visible affordances

Çamcı et al. recommend tiered control: expressive low-level actions (grab, move) with richer controls behind them [5]. Neo Theremin, Wavr and Handel all offer a few modes rather than a blank canvas [8][9]; Table Piano offers gaze-pinch and poke side by side [42]; PianoVision fades its guidance as the player learns [40]. For thoremin this maps onto its existing machinery: a **seed** per mode (theremin, chord pad, draw), a **slice** for each learning level, and a **lazy extension** for rarely used richer controls. The visual layer should show what each hand will do (a pitch ruler, a ghost of the drawing area) before the player acts, as `canvas-overlay` already does for pitch guides. In XR, patching metaphors (Patchworld, LyraVR) show a graph can be a *playground* rather than a diagram [33][38]; the typed DAG's ports could be rendered as such, but that is a research bet, not a lesson from evidence.

### 3.5 Quantisation versus expressiveness: one dial, not one choice

Electronauts quantises to a licensed track and sounds good at once [36]; Virtuoso offers scale presets and tempo sync [37]; Neo Theremin has note snapping [8]; Kandinsky quantises timing to a loop and pitch to a scale [30]; Paint with Music keeps continuous pitch and adds stroke speed as dynamics [31]; Oramics and UPIC draw continuous curves [23][25]. Thoremin already has magnetic scale snapping in `src/music/theory.ts` (see `docs/ARCHITECTURE.md`). The design lesson is that the dial belongs to the **player and the seed**: a beginner seed with strong snapping and a pre-harmonised bed, an advanced seed with free glide and continuous curves, and the same graph. Preserve the continuous value and snap in a separate node so both can be recorded.

### 3.6 Playing a drawing

Every drawn-sound system above uses the same four decisions, and thoremin should make each an explicit node parameter:

1. **Axes.** Time on x, pitch on y (UPIC, Oramics, Kandinsky, Paint with Music) [23][25][30][31]. In AR the drawing may be on a surface or in free 3D; MagneTip draws in 3D [6]. A 2D plane in front of the player (a "drawing wall") fits a webcam's view; depth can map to a third parameter (timbre, as Animoog Galaxy does with z) [41].
2. **Reading.** A playhead scanning at a tempo (loop) is the common choice; the alternative is the player's own hand as the playhead ("scrub"). The loop gives hands-free repetition, which also cuts fatigue (3.3).
3. **Stroke as score.** Strokes carry more than position: speed can map to volume (Paint with Music) [31], colour or shape to timbre (Kandinsky, Hyperscore) [27][30], thickness to loudness (Oramics volume graphs) [24]. Record the stroke as a timestamped polyline so later mappings can change.
4. **Editing as playing.** Oram drew melody, then dynamics, then vibrato and reverb in separate passes [24]. A thoremin "layer" per parameter (draw the melody, then draw a volume contour over it) is a direct translation, and is where "draw and move objects" becomes music rather than decoration.

**Cost and fit.** A drawing is a new sensor-derived data type (a stroke list) and a new output node (playhead reader) in the DAG, plus a canvas layer; no change to the engine. It would be a natural **lazy extension** (a "draw" seed), loaded on demand like thoremin-asl, since its UI code is large and not every player wants it.

### 3.7 Things to avoid, and open questions

- Do not promise headset-quality registration on a webcam: virtual objects will not be world-locked, only screen-locked. Glasses change that; a note on the Meta path is in section 4.
- Do not rely on depth from one camera for poke events; use a surface plane or pinch instead (Leap's reach gesture [18] needed a depth sensor).
- Open: no hand-tracking-only 3D musical drawing was found [6][32]; whether pinch-draw is playable at 30 fps without fatigue is unmeasured and should be a thoremin experiment (strokes per minute, error versus a template, CE score).
- Open: no peer-reviewed latency study for a hand-tracked *instrument* on Quest 3 or Vision Pro was found; the three figures above are general hand-tracking latencies.

## 4. A note on Meta glasses

Meta opened developer access to the Ray-Ban Display glasses in a developer preview in May 2026, with a native mobile toolkit (iOS, Android) and a Web Apps path (HTML, CSS, JavaScript deployed by URL), and Neural Band EMG gestures (pinch, scroll) as input; camera access is a separate capability of the Wearables Device Access Toolkit, display sessions are user-initiated and exclusive, and both paths may change before a stable release [13]. For thoremin that suggests the Neural Band's pinch is a good pitch/volume gate and an EMG complement to vision, which is the combination Graf and Barthet argue improves occluded fingers [4]. Whether the glasses' camera stream can feed MediaPipe at usable latency was not checked here.
